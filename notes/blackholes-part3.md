| The answer is B: you get stretched ("spaghettification") | Tidal forces stretch you lengthwise and squeeze you sideways; the term was popularized by Hawking | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaghettification |
| Your feet get pulled much harder than your head | Tidal acceleration a = 2GMd/R³; feet-first, the gravity at your feet is stronger than at your head | https://www.astronomy.com/science/ask-astro-why-are-small-black-holes-more-dangerous-than-big-ones/ |

| A giant black hole: you cross the edge and feel almost nothing | Supermassive black holes: the deadly point lies inside the horizon, so you can cross it without noticing tidal forces | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaghettification |
| "about 10 suns heavy" / "millions of suns heavy" | Stellar black hole ~10 solar masses (horizon ~30 km); Sgr A* ~4.3 million solar masses | https://ifs.uoregon.edu/?p=2096, https://www.sciencealert.com/nasas-stunning-new-simulation-sends-you-diving-into-a-black-hole |
| ~13 s after crossing, it's over (NASA simulation) | NASA simulation based on a black hole like Sgr A*: camera spaghettified 12.8 s after crossing the horizon | https://www.sciencealert.com/nasas-stunning-new-simulation-sends-you-diving-into-a-black-hole |

Deliberate simplifications:
- "Feel almost nothing" refers only to the stretching at the edge. Radiation and hot gas around the black hole are not covered.
- "About 13 seconds" comes from one NASA simulation, not a general rule.
- Burn (A) is not entirely wrong: the hot gas disk around a feeding black hole could also be deadly. The video treats "stretch" as the answer to "what the black hole itself does to you".
